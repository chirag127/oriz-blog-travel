# Solo travel safety in India: a practical field checklist

Solo travel in India is done safely by millions of people every year, including
first-timers and women travelling alone. The advice that circulates online tends
to swing between two useless extremes: "it is perfectly fine, go anywhere" and
"never leave your hotel." Neither helps you make decisions at 11pm at a bus
stand in a town you have never been to.

What follows is the checklist I actually run — the habits that lower risk
without shrinking your trip into a hotel room.

## Before you leave: set up the boring safety net

- **Share a live location** with one person who will notice if it stops moving.
  WhatsApp live location or Google Maps location sharing both work. Agree on a
  daily check-in time, not a vague "I'll message when I can."
- **Photograph your documents** and mail them to yourself: passport or Aadhaar,
  the page with your visa if you have one, and your travel insurance number.
- **Keep money in two places.** A day's cash in your pocket, the rest and a
  spare card zipped somewhere separate. If a wallet goes, the trip does not.
- **Save the numbers that matter:** 112 (national emergency), 1091 (women's
  helpline), 1363 (tourist helpline), and the number of your accommodation.

None of this is exciting. All of it is what turns a bad hour into an
inconvenience instead of a crisis.

## Transport: the leg where most trouble starts

Getting between places is where solo travellers are most exposed, because you
are tired, carrying everything you own, and often arriving after dark.

- **Prefer app-based cabs** (Ola, Uber, Rapido) over street autos at night —
  the ride is logged, the driver is identified, and you can share the trip.
- **Screenshot the number plate** and send it to your check-in person before
  you get in, especially for a pre-arranged pickup you did not book on an app.
- **Sit behind the driver, not beside.** Keep your bag on your lap or between
  your feet, not in the boot, for short rides.
- **On overnight trains and buses,** book upper berths where you can, keep your
  bag chained or looped through the frame, and trust the crowd — Indian trains
  are busy and that is a safety feature, not a nuisance.

Arriving somewhere new after midnight is the single avoidable risk. When you can
choose the timing, arrive in daylight. It is worth a wasted half-day.

## Reading a situation early

Most uncomfortable encounters give you warning signs before they escalate. The
skill is noticing them and acting while the exit is still cheap and easy.

- **Trust the first flinch.** If a guesthouse, a driver, or an offer feels off,
  you do not owe anyone an explanation for walking away.
- **Keep your plans vague with strangers.** "Meeting friends here" is a
  complete answer. You do not need to announce that you are travelling alone or
  where you are staying.
- **Use public and busy over quiet and private** when you are unsure — a
  crowded chai stall, a station platform, a shop. Ask a family or a woman with
  children for directions before a lone man.
- **Have a "no" ready** that does not negotiate. A firm, unbothered refusal
  ends most persistent approaches faster than politeness does.

## For women travelling alone, specifically

The above applies to everyone; a few things bear extra weight. Dress in a way
that lets you move without drawing comment in the region you are in — that is
about blending in, not about blame. Choose accommodation with reviews from other
solo women where you can; hostel and homestay reviews are candid about this.
Ladies' compartments on trains and the front seats reserved for women on buses
exist for a reason — use them. And the women's helpline, 1091, connects to a
dedicated response in most states.

## When something does go wrong

Get to a public, lit, busy place first. Call 112 — it works from any phone,
even without a SIM or balance, and routes to police, fire, or ambulance. If you
are robbed, prioritise getting somewhere safe over recovering the item; file the
report afterward, which you will need for insurance anyway. Tell your check-in
person immediately so someone offline knows.

The honest summary: India is not uniquely dangerous, and it is not uniformly
safe. It rewards travellers who stay alert on the transport legs, arrive in
daylight, and keep one person informed. Do those three things and the rest of
the trip is mostly just the good part.
